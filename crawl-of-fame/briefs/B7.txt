WHERE WE ARE: look at /home/user/shots/B5/tour_sheet.png, looks_sheet_a.png and looks_sheet_b.png first. Floor 1 has a coherent kit and good props, corridors and green rooms work. The lead's review of what is still wrong, in order of importance:
(a) Sameness. Every room is the same low brown hall: one plank floor, one ceiling height of five blocks, one grid of hanging lamps, props sprinkled on a regular grid. Rooms of two by two and three by three tiles (32 to 48 blocks wide) read as warehouses with repeated clutter (hundreds of chairs or flight cases in rows).
(b) No large shapes. Nothing breaks a big room into spaces: no partitions, no height steps, no centre piece. Fights have little to play around.
(c) Flat light. Lamps on a uniform grid light everything the same.
(d) Brown on brown. Brick, dark timber and dark boards everywhere; colour only in the district band.
(e) Floors 2 and 3 are still vanilla palettes in the floor 1 shell.

DELIVER
1. Volume. The ceiling height follows the room: small rooms stay low, rooms of two tiles get 7 to 8, the largest 9 to 11, with what such a hall needs: trusses or beams with hanging lamps on chains, a catwalk or gallery on one side that can be walked (stairs up, a loot crate or a marksman's perch on it), high windows of the "outside" painted on flats. Split-level floors: stages, sunken pits, loading ramps, mezzanines; every level reachable on foot by players and by walking monsters (the director needs standing room on the main level).
2. Structure in big rooms. Painters compose large rooms from zones, not from a sprinkle: partition walls and half walls with openings, rows of columns, scenery flats (painted backdrops on frames, the back side raw timber), stacked crate walls, curtains in other colours as soft walls, so that a 48 block hall becomes three or four connected spaces with sight lines broken and at least two ways around. One centre piece per large room that a player remembers (a half built set of a living room, a ship's bow, a castle gate of plywood, a giant prop, a lighting rig lowered to the floor, a wardrobe maze...). At least 16 combat looks on floor 1 after this, each clearly different in silhouette, not only in props; the chair rows and the flight case fields become parts of looks, never a whole hall.
3. Light as direction. Fewer, purposeful sources: pools on set pieces, doors and loot, darker stretches between; spotlights on centre pieces; lit signs; where players fight the floor is still readable (never below light 7 on main walkways, measure it in a game test).
4. Colour. Scenery flats and props bring it in: painted skies, forests, city skylines, costumes, coloured velvet, hazard yellow, tape marks, big billboards of invented shows; the district colour also on door frames and pipe paint. Draw what the kit lacks (own textures as before, nothing copied).
5. The boss arenas become stages worth a title card: a proscenium with gold trim and footlights, a marked centre, an audience on the gallery (seat rows with cardboard cut-out spectators as props), spotlights from above, a different back drop per district faction of the floor.
6. Floor 2 "Prop Storage" and floor 3 "The Backlot" get their own dressing in this language, each with its own surfaces (own textures), fixtures, prop sets, 12 or more combat looks and special rooms, clearly not floor 1 recoloured. Floor 2: a prop warehouse at night: concrete and steel shelving up to the ceiling with labelled crates, cage walls, a forklift, aisles marked on the floor, mannequin storage, cold strip lights, loading docks. Floor 3: a fake town in a cavern, built to be filmed: streets with facades of several styles (western, noir city, village), a town square with a dry fountain, shop fronts whose doors lead into small interiors, street lamps, a painted sky dome with visible seams and scaffolding behind the facades, parked camera cranes. Their monsters must fit in (mannequins among mannequins, extras on the streets).
